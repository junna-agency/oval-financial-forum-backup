The Month in Brief

Market Overview

Many investors entered October with a strong sense of optimism. The S&P 500 (SPX) and Nasdaq Composite (IXIC) reached record highs, and artificial intelligence continued to attract significant investment. Investors were preparing for the third-quarter earnings season, with some of the largest companies in the world reporting their financial results. As the month went on, this optimism began to fade. Questions surrounding valuations of technology stocks, rising energy prices, and the future of interest rates began to rise. By the end of the month, the Nasdaq had fallen 5.30%, and the S&P 500 fell 0.93%. Meanwhile, the Dow Jones managed to gain 1.70%, as investors began looking beyond technology in search of stronger returns.

As was the case throughout much of 2026, AI remained a major focus for investors in October. At the end of the month, a large tech sell-off took place following disappointing earnings reports from companies such as Google and Amazon. Nvidia (NVDA) reached an all-time high of $243.37 per share on October 6. Meanwhile, Google’s (GOOGL) 20-year energy agreement with Constellation Energy (CEG) showed investors just how much electricity the company expects to need as it continues investing in AI over the next two decades. Around the middle of October, Anthropic announced partnerships with Workday and HCLTech, showing its efforts to expand agentic AI capabilities to bring these tools into daily operations. Despite these developments, investor concerns over rising AI spending continued, particularly surrounding whether these massive investments would generate enough profit to justify their costs.

The bond market also had a big impact on investor sentiment throughout October. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.31% on October 5, its highest closing level since 2002, putting additional pressure on stocks already trading at elevated valuations. Due to the heightened yields at the beginning of the month, highly volatile technology stocks were less appealing as investors rotated into the bond market. During the week ending October 7, U.S. bond funds attracted a record $19.78 billion in inflows, while equity funds experienced $5.11 billion in outflows. By the end of October, the 10-year Treasury yield had fallen to 4.93%, following the Federal Reserve’s October 28 decision to hold its benchmark interest rate at 3.75% to 4.00%.

Oil was another focal point in the month of October, as a result of the rising geopolitical tensions in the Middle East and concerns about supply disruptions. On October 13, attacks on vessels in the Strait of Hormuz highlighted the risks facing one of the world’s most important oil shipping routes. These concerns intensified toward the end of the month following additional attacks on oil tankers and threats against Saudi Arabian oil infrastructure. WTI crude futures finished October at $104.12 per barrel, a 15.15% increase for the month and its highest closing level since April.

Commodities such as gold, silver, and Bitcoin (BTC-USD) performed poorly during October; silver had the most difficult month, falling 18.31%. Investors became progressively impatient with the technology sector as companies continued pouring billions into AI development, making investors wonder about when these investments would translate into stronger earnings and positive cash flow. With so much uncertainty in the market, investors seemed less willing to take risks. This was reflected in the Cboe Volatility Index, which rose 18.9% throughout October, signaling growing expectations of market volatility. As October came to a close, investors looked ahead to the upcoming midterm elections while the technology sector faced growing pressure to restore market confidence.

The Macro Picture

Macroeconomic & Geopolitical Overview

Macro

October’s macroeconomic backdrop was defined by a Federal Reserve pause, a bond rally off multi-decade highs, and a tech-led pullback in equities. On October 28, the FOMC voted 10–2 to hold the federal funds target range at 3.75%–4.00% after September’s hike, with two of the committee members dissenting in favor of a 25-basis-point increase. Elevated inflation strengthened the case for the dissenters’ position. September CPI rose to 3.7% year-over-year, up from 3.4% in August, while September PCE rose to 3.6% from 3.4%, and core PCE held above 3% at 3.1%. Energy remained a key driver as the U.S.-Iran conflict kept oil elevated, with Brent ending the month above $117 per barrel.

Despite the hawkish tilt, the bond market turned things around. The 10-year treasury fell 36 basis points from 5.29% on September 30 to 4.93% on October 30, after peaking at 5.35% on October 5. The 30-year yield fell 33 basis points from 5.64% to 5.31%, after hitting 5.73% on October 7. Late in the month, a wave of AI stock selloffs sent investors toward Treasuries, pushing the yields lower.

Equity leadership flipped from September. The Nasdaq fell 5.3%, its worst month since March 2025, while the S&P 500 dipped 0.9%. The Dow Jones Industrial Average gained 1.7%, and the Russell 2000 gained 0.5%. The S&P 500 reached a 52-week high of 7,844.52 on October 6 before earnings from Amazon, Alphabet, and Intel renewed concerns about AI spending. On October 30, the Nasdaq had its worst day since June, falling 2.9% to 25,437. Tech and communication services were the only S&P 500 sectors to decline in October, while ExxonMobil and Chevron hit record highs as their refining margins grew. Truist’s CIO said the weakness was tech-concentrated rather than spread across the market, calling it a rotation rather than a broad selloff. This doesn’t guarantee a rebound, but it shows the pullback was concentrated in one part of the market.

Commodities moved in opposite directions. Oil extended its rally from September with Brent rising 13.6%, closing out the month at $117.64 per barrel. Gold and silver both fell, 6.9% and 18.3% respectively, with strong economic data and anticipation of further Fed rate hikes likely weighing on the precious metals. The U.S. dollar index rose 0.4%, slightly increasing the cost of American commodities for foreign buyers. Bitcoin finished October down 7.3% at $77,434.75, with a record-breaking Binance security breach adding to late-month uncertainty.

The labor market rebounded in October with nonfarm payrolls rising by 165,000, more than triple the 12-month average gain of 48,000. The unemployment rate edged down from 4.2% to 4.1%, staying within the narrow range it has held since March. Upward revisions added a combined 84,000 jobs to August and September, improving the weak picture from September. Wage growth, at 3.1% year-over-year, remains below the latest inflation readings, meaning wages are still not keeping pace with the rising cost of living. For the Fed, stronger hiring supports the case for further rate hikes, while moderate wage growth suggests pay isn’t adding fuel to inflation yet.

The next big test is the Fed’s meeting on December 8–9. On October 30, St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem and Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin both suggested that rates may need to rise further to bring down inflation, and, following the release of the September PCE data, the markets are leaning toward a December rate hike. Speaking in New York on October 30, Musalem stated, “I judge that additional firming of monetary policy will be necessary, and soon.” October CPI data arrives on November 10 and October PCE follows on November 25. Oil, which remains tied to the Iran conflict, will continue adding uncertainty to the Fed’s next move.

Geo

Iran & The Strait of Hormuz

Much of October was highlighted by conflict in The Strait Of Hormuz, as tensions with Iran continued. Brent Crude rose 13.6% during the month and settled at $117.64 a barrel on October 30. A fleet of tankers protected by the U.S. helped push Gulf oil exports back near prewar levels. The heightened prices were unexpected given the restored supply, but it suggests markets placed more emphasis on risk of disruption rather than actual supply.

September 25 marked nearly seven months of ongoing conflict between the U.S. and Iran, which began February 28. Iran offered to reopen the strait within seven days if the U.S. lifted its naval blockade, waived sanctions on Iranian oil sales, and observed a ceasefire that included Lebanon. President Trump rejected the offer in late September. On October 22, Supreme National Security Council Secretary Mohsen Rezaei stated that Tehran was “no longer interested in agreements whose only guarantee was political good faith.” The stalemate persisted through the end of October with Foreign Minister Araghchi claiming that a proposal discussed with mediators could have reopened the strait within two days. U.S. officials denied any breakthrough, directly contradicting the Minister’s statement.

October saw the highest number of reported attacks on commercial shipping since the beginning of the war, according to the U.K. Maritime Trade Operations agency. Houthi attacks on Saudi airports and facilities threatened the Bab el-Mandeb. The impact of this conflict spans far beyond gas prices as thousands of lives were lost. Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, which has increased pipeline capacity to 7 million barrels per day according to Aramco’s president, serves as the primary alternative route for a closed Hormuz. This route has been continuously threatened and is what has made shipping oil from the Middle East so difficult throughout the conflict.

Russia-Ukraine

Since President Biden’s banning of Russian crude oil, petroleum, liquefied natural gas, and coal in March of 2022, there have been no recent developments on restoring imports. This Executive Order was signed in response to the Russia-Ukraine War. This began to change in October when the United States and Russia struck a deal to import 300,000 tons of diesel immediately, 500,000 tons in November, then 1 million and 3 million more. On October 9, The United States Treasury also issued a license that suspended sanctions until April 2027 on Russian diesel.

Daniel Sternoff, a senior fellow at the Columbia Center on Global Energy Policy, spoke on global energy policy, saying “Refined products like diesel are still barely half of prewar levels.” Additionally, Clayton Seigle of the Center for Strategic and International Studies said the deal is unlikely to materially lower U.S. or European prices but relieves pressure on Moscow’s revenue.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy referred to it as “a weak decision, unfortunately, a weak decision by strong partners.”

Russia banned diesel exports in July after Ukrainian drone strikes cut its output by about 30%. Zelenskyy said Ukraine would stop striking Russian refineries only if Russia stopped attacking Ukraine’s power grid. He stated clearly, “if they want [an] energy ceasefire, we are open for this.”

U.S.-China Relations

In last month’s edition, we covered the Trump-Xi summit which extended the trade truce until January 10. While this truce covered tariffs, questions remain over technology disputes. On October 16, China’s Commerce and Science Ministries proposed restricting or banning exports of 40 technologies, including 23 in AI, 12 in solar and 5 in biomedicine. A ban would cover AI models trained on sensitive national data and AI agents built for defense or critical infrastructure. Restrictions could apply to AI models with 2 trillion or more parameters. The AP notes Moonshot’s Kimi K3 and Alibaba’s Qwen 3.8-Max exceed this level but the initial proposal did not name specific models. The Chinese Ministry cited national security and keeping AI under human control. This proposal came while the two countries were making progress on AI safety talks. China was cooperating on AI safety and restricting exports at the same time. Gary Ng, senior economist at Natixis, said the world is becoming “more fragmented in trade and investment, but also in access to technologies.”

A Homeland Security notice made public on October 19 directed federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek products. The U.S. fears that DeepSeek’s servers, located in China, could potentially access sensitive U.S. data. China’s Commerce Ministry noted that the U.S. had disregarded the consensus reached by President Xi and President Trump. It also promised all necessary measures in response. George Chen, partner at The Asia Group, said China’s warnings reflect “the depth of strategic distrust between Beijing and Washington” even with the two presidents having a seemingly positive relationship.

In October, multiple major foreign-policy decisions coincided with the campaign calendar. On October 8, President Trump stated in a Truth Social post that the U.S. would not attack Iran before November 3. The AP notes that the President has stated multiple times that post-election escalation remains an option. On October 9, President Trump announced a diesel deal with Russia which aimed to alleviate some of the high fuel prices ahead of the midterms. On October 16, Trump waived sanctions on Russia that were to go into effect only a few days later. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that the administration needed about 60 days to study the economic effects. Republican Senator Thom Tillis from North Carolina shared his thoughts in saying that the move was “beyond irresponsible” and pointed out that the law had passed the Senate 86-11. The volatility index, VIX, closed September 30 at 16.34 and October 30 at 19.42, up about 19% and above the long-term median of 17.6. While the VIX rose, stocks barely moved. The S&P 500 closed down 0.9%. During October, fear rose more than prices fell. With the pre-election pause on Iran strikes and the 60-day review of Russia sanctions both running past November 3, the question is if these decisions will hold.

Sector Analysis

The Energy Sector

The energy sector experienced significant growth in October, gaining 13.8% as measured by the S&P 500 energy sector index, rebounding from September’s decline. The sector’s recent surge stems from rising oil prices amid the continued conflict in the Middle East. Brent futures oil prices closed the month of October at $117.64, a 13.63% rise from September’s $103.53 price. The increase in prices benefits integrated oil companies such as Chevron (CVX) and ExxonMobil (XOM) who make up about 48.81% of the S&P 500’s energy sector market capitalization. The other 19 companies within the sector experienced an average growth of 12.7%, almost matching the sector average. This climb reflects increased activity within oilfield servicing, exploration, and production, as rising prices encourage producers to expand operations and increase investments in domestic energy development.

ConocoPhillips (COP) and EOG Resources (EOG) led the energy sector through their specialization in exploration and production of oil, with gains of 23% and 21%, respectively. This highlights the strength of upstream companies, which have more direct exposure to changes in commodity prices. In ConocoPhillips’ third-quarter results, its average realized crude oil price increased by 34% year-over-year to $88.80 per barrel. This helped drive net income to $4 billion, more than double the $1.7 billion reported the previous year, despite a 4% decline in production. Similarly, EOG Resources reported strong third-quarter results. Its average realized crude oil price increased approximately 31% year-over-year to $86.72 per barrel, compared to $65.95 the previous year. Unlike ConocoPhillips, EOG Resources’ production increased around 10.1%, bringing revenue to $8.55 billion. Together, these results show how rising oil prices benefit upstream producers despite production moving in different directions, explaining why exploration and production companies were among the strongest performers in October’s energy sector rally.

Alongside oil, natural gas has been a major contributor to the total energy consumption in the US. The Dow Jones Commodity Index Natural Gas (DJCI Natural Gas) dropped 4.23% during the month, caused by warmer-than-expected weather and strong domestic production. According to the NOAA, October 2026 was the fourth-warmest October in US history, with an average temperature of 58.7 degrees Fahrenheit, decreasing the month’s heating demands. Meanwhile, domestic dry natural gas production remained strong, with average output through October 29 up 4% year-over-year. Higher production also led to higher storage inventories. As of October 30, underground storage had reached 3,801 billion cubic feet (Bcf), approximately 1% above the five-year average. These conditions coupled with warmer weather contributed to the decline in DJCI Natural Gas.

Special Segment

AI and Nuclear Energy

Since the late 1970s, nuclear power has been defined by its disasters: Three Mile Island in 1979, Chernobyl in 1986, and Fukushima in 2011. Together, these historical catastrophes turned an engineering masterpiece into a threat to public safety, a source of environmental contamination, and a political third rail. Germany switched off its reactors, and America went more than 30 years without approving a new one.

Fast forward to today’s hottest topic: artificial intelligence. After shutting down in 2019 due to competition from low-cost gas, Three Mile Island’s Unit 1 reactor (now named Crane Clean Energy Center) will resume operating in 2028 to fulfill data center power demands in the PJM, a regional grid that serves 67 million people from Illinois to Virginia.

Although nuclear energy still raises public concern, the surge in electricity demand driven by artificial intelligence is driving new efforts to expand nuclear power production.

Five years ago, a data center that drew 50 megawatts was considered large. Today data centers require 30-500 megawatts on average, with large AI-focused facilities using 1000 megawatts (1 gigawatt) or more for operations. Large tech companies, known as hyperscalers, are planning data center “campuses” 100 times that size, each costing up to $200 billion.

The price for energy is growing alongside the increase in AI power demand. BloombergNEF expects American data centers to use more than twice as much power by 2034 as they did last year. The growing cost is sharpest in the PJM region. There, projections show that data centers could go from using a tenth of all PJM electricity capacity to more than a quarter.

Locating power is one of the major headwinds for AI data center buildout. For context, a data center can be constructed and connected to the US grid in roughly two to five years. The US grid provides consistent flows of energy into data centers. In contrast, connecting a new power plant to the US grid now takes four to five years, twice as long as in 2008. Although demand for AI infrastructure such as GPUs, ASICs, and optical connectivity remains significant, the most urgent requirement for functionality is electricity.

Since AI data centers run around the clock, it is crucial to find a source of energy that can run continuously at any given point. Data centers also come with environmental obligations, which rule out leaning on fossil fuels for long. Wind and solar are clean but intermittent, running roughly a quarter to a third of their maximum output; on the other hand, gas is steady but emits carbon. Nuclear is one of the few proven sources that is steady, clean and scalable. Nuclear plants account for less than 8% of the U.S. grid’s total generating capacity, yet they supply 19% of the nation’s electricity. Nuclear plants run at 92% of their generating capacity as a result of their nonstop power generation, whereas wind and solar only run at around 25% to 35% of their generating capacity due to fluctuations in weather.

Inside a nuclear reactor, uranium atoms are split apart through nuclear fission. The released heat is used to produce steam, which spins the turbine.

The resulting heat source is remarkably dense. A uranium pellet about the size of a fingertip holds as much energy as a ton of coal, and splitting it releases no carbon dioxide.

Generating the power is only half the problem. Getting it to the data center is the other.

Most data center centric deals today take the indirect route. The reactor supplies the grid with power, while a hyperscaler signs a long-term contract (often 20 years) for a productive amount of carbon-free electricity.

The faster option is to plug straight in. A data center built beside a reactor can draw power directly, skipping years in the queue for a grid connection. But regulators have pushed back, arguing that such deals let giant customers avoid paying for a grid everyone else funds. New rules allow these links only if data centers pay for 100% of the costs for the new grid infrastructure built to serve it.

A relatively new method for nuclear power generation is becoming increasingly more popular for data center buildout. This method involves providing a data center with a reactor of its own. Small modular reactors (SMRs) are built for exactly that job.

Today’s nuclear options typically produce 800 megawatts or more and can take a decade to build. SMRs produce 300 megawatts or less, but take only two to three years to build; they are also designed to be built in factories and shipped to site in pieces.

Major tech companies such as Google and Meta are investing in SMR energy production. On October 14, 2024, Google agreed to buy up to 500 megawatts from Kairos Power by 2035. Meta plans to build a 1.2 gigawatt location only 70 miles away from Columbus in Pike County, built from 16 of Oklo’s reactors. Oklo (OKLO) and Kairos Power are two companies that produce these SMRs and are on track to make significant progress while data center power demand continues to grow.

The catch is that the technology is still largely a promise. Only two SMRs produce commercial power anywhere in the world, one in Russia and one in China. North America’s first is not due until the end of 2030. Nuclear reactors also require a stronger grade of uranium fuel that North America barely produces, and the U.S. already imports more than 95% of its uranium supply.

For owners of existing nuclear reactors, tech companies have arrived at the perfect moment. Constellation Energy (CEG) is spending $1.6 billion to restart Three Mile Island’s Unit 1, backed by a 20-year Power Purchase Agreement to sell its output to Microsoft. A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) is a long-term contract (typically 5 to 30 years) where a third party developer (hyperscalers in this case) installs, owns and maintains an energy system. Google is backing $4.3 billion in upgrades to unlock an extra 890 megawatts of power from 11 existing nuclear reactors. Amazon has signed an $18 billion PPA for Susquehanna’s power through 2042. Susquehanna Steam Electric Station is a nuclear plant in Pennsylvania that powers over two million homes.

Restarting about 2 gigawatts of closed power plants costs roughly $6 billion, according to Deloitte. Building the same capacity costs about $37 billion. Old reactors are the cheapest clean megawatts available, and there are only so many of them.

Alternatives such as Liquefied Natural Gases (LNGs) are quicker and easier to build, yet the legislation is becoming increasingly politicized. Even now, local opposition blocked or delayed $130 billion in data center projects during the first quarter of this year alone.

Nuclear’s newest technology is moving faster than skeptics expected. This summer, four advanced test reactors achieved a sustained chain reaction in Idaho, beating a White House deadline. One developer is now building a 10-megawatt unit to power a data center in 2027.

The real test comes around 2030, when the first commercial SMRs are expected to reach the grid. If SMRs arrive with cost efficiency, the next wave of contracts becomes financeable. If they slip, capital will drift back to gas.

In the Courts

Corporate Litigation

Publicly and privately traded companies frequently face litigation. This month, there are two main cases to pay attention to.

Suncor Energy (U.S.A.) Inc. v. County Commissioners of Boulder County

On October 5, the Supreme Court heard oral arguments regarding Boulder, Colorado’s lawsuit against Exxon Mobil (XOM) and Suncor Energy (SU). The case raises the question of whether federal law preempts state-law claims seeking compensation for local harms allegedly caused by greenhouse gas emissions. It essentially asks if state law can be used to seek remedies in the case even though federal regulations around climate change already exist. The Court must also determine whether it has jurisdiction to review the Colorado Supreme Court’s decision at this stage of the litigation. If the Supreme Court rules in favor of Boulder on the preemption question, the case can proceed in Colorado state court, where the underlying claims will then be litigated.

The oil companies and the US government, in an amicus brief (something parties impacted by a lawsuit but not involved can file), argue that deciding in favor of Boulder would generate greater risk of future litigation for fossil fuel producers, which could lead to underproduction. Additionally, the lawsuit raises broader constitutional questions involving horizontal federalism, as states are generally prohibited from directly regulating activities outside their borders. The case also involves interstate and international emissions, meaning there could be foreign affairs and national interests the federal government should handle.

The defendants, Boulder County and the City of Boulder, sued the companies, alleging that their actions knowingly contributed to climate change and caused harm to residents. The companies are also facing accusations of misleading the public about the role of fossil fuels in climate change. The underlying merits have not been decided at trial since the questions before the Supreme Court must be answered first.

Oral arguments do not appear to have affected the stock price of Exxon Mobil or Suncor Energy. From October 2 to October 5, Exxon’s shares didn’t change at all and Suncor Energy dropped 1.14%. Earlier, in February, when certiorari was granted on Feb 23, stock prices had barely moved. February 20 to 23, Exxon Mobil actually went up 2.23% and Suncor Energy increased 0.95%. One reason for this is that, at this point, investors may already be factoring in the threat of climate litigation into the stock price. SCOTUS’s opinion, potential litigation at the state level, or a settlement may have a greater impact on stock price moving forward, however.

Federal Trade Commission (and state partners) v. Dollar General Corporation

In an inflationary environment, will demand for inferior goods boost Dollar General stock price, or will the impending threat of the FTC supported by 20 states, including Ohio, hurt the stock price? While stocks seem to have bounced back since the October 26, 2026 FTC complaint filing, there was an initial reaction from investors. From Oct 22, 2026 to Oct 28, 2026, Dollar General’s stock fell from $139.11 to $126.17, a 9.3% drop. Not all of this can be attributed to the FTC’s lawsuit, but much of the dip occurred in the same timeframe.

The FTC is suing Dollar General over claims that it has charged customers more at checkout than the prices displayed on store shelves, product labels, and advertisements. According to the FTC’s filing, Dollar General has allegedly received tens of thousands of nationwide complaints and has failed to correct this issue. If the allegations are true, this would violate the FTC Act, Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and state laws. Should the suit be successful, Dollar General could be required to compensate those affected, cutting into their bottom line.

Corporate litigation often takes years or ends in a settlement, so consumers won’t have answers for a while, but in the meantime, this stock has already shown increased volatility since the suit was filed.

IPO Watch

Anthropic

Following its recently amended November 2 S-1 Filing, AI company Anthropic (ANT) has drawn serious attention from both investors and the general public as the company prepares for its highly anticipated initial public offering. Its filing revealed strong financial growth, reporting third quarter revenue of $16.96 billion, a 1,127% increase year-over-year and a 47% rise from the previous quarter. Despite this, Anthropic still faces challenges surrounding its infrastructure spending, as well as its ability to maintain profitability. As skepticism around AI increases, investors are questioning whether the company will be able to justify its valuation.

The most significant driver of Anthropic’s revenue growth has been the adoption of its Claude models by customers. Unlike other AI companies which rely mainly on monthly subscriptions, Anthropic generates a large portion of its revenue through a consumption-based model it calls “pay-for-intelligence.” Under this system, businesses and consumers pay to use Anthropic’s technology according to their level of usage, which is measured in AI tokens. This business model has proven successful for Anthropic. Consumption-based revenue reached $26.3 billion during the first three quarters of 2026, which represented about 79% of its total revenue. Subscription services, which include paid access to Claude’s applications, generated another $6.9 billion, accounting for the remaining 21%.

Anthropic’s growth in revenue can also be attributed to its increasing enterprise customer base, with their Claude tools being directly implemented by businesses. Its products include Claude Code, an AI-powered coding assistant that helps developers write and maintain software, as well as Claude Cowork, which assists users with professional tasks like research and presentations. As companies establish a greater reliance on these applications, Anthropic benefits not only from the acquisition of new customers, but also from its existing customers’ expanding usage. According to its S-1 Filing, Anthropic had more than 11,900 customers generating at least $100,000 in trailing-twelve-month revenue as of September 30, 2026, compared to around 950 customers in the previous year.

Anthropic’s initial public offering is set to release the week before Thanksgiving. Following its $65 billion Series H funding round in May, the company reached a private valuation of $965 billion. Initial expectations for the company’s public debut were extremely optimistic, with some investor estimates suggesting a valuation upwards of $2 trillion, surpassing SpaceX’s $1.77 trillion valuation earlier this year. However, enthusiasm for Anthropic has since cooled as skepticism has grown surrounding the future of AI investments, as well as the company’s ability to maintain long-term profitability. Anthropic has continued to aggressively reinvest in model development and compute in order to meet the growing demand within the AI market. Despite its solid Q3 performance, Anthropic’s revenue growth has begun to slow, decreasing from a 144% quarter-over-quarter growth in Q2 to 47% in Q3. Anthropic also reported a Q3 operating loss of $567 million, which was largely driven by the high costs of computing infrastructure needed to operate and train its models.

While this capital-intensive strategy has primarily fueled the company’s recent growth, it has also raised concerns about the sustainability of Anthropic’s business model. Anthropic, along with other AI companies, has committed billions of dollars toward infrastructure and development. As skepticism around AI grows, investors are hesitant on whether Anthropic’s enormous valuation can be justified, as well as the profitability of the AI industry as a whole. Prospective investors have recently valued Anthropic more conservatively, ranging from $1.3 trillion to $1.5 trillion as of the end of October.

As Anthropic approaches its public debut, its IPO will serve as a major test on whether investor enthusiasm for AI can overcome the mounting speculation surrounding industry spending, as well as maintaining long-term profitability.

New Listings

IPO Roundup

October at a Glance

IPO activity was quiet during October: 8 IPOs raised a total of $1.2 billion, plus one direct listing (a company listing existing shares without selling new ones), well below the 10-year October average of 22 IPOs raising $5.1 billion. Biotech companies led the way, accounting for 7 of the 9 listings.

The results were mixed. October’s traditional IPOs (excluding the direct listing) averaged 8% from the offer price; however, nearly two-thirds were subpar, trading below their offer prices this month. A few large winners, such as Lycia, boosted the average and masked the small losses. However, in October, the Renaissance IPO Index, which tracks newly public companies, fell 10% while the S&P 500 fell only 1%.

Renaissance highlighted concerns about AI spending, resumed rate hikes (the Fed’s September hike), and bond yields reaching a 19-year high, which have weighed on IPO activity and performance this fall.

Notable IPO Entries

The month’s largest deal and two late-month biotech debuts

Lycia Therapeutics (LYCA) · Listed on Nasdaq on 10/23

What they do: Early-stage biotech (drugs are still in testing) developing therapies to clear disease-causing proteins. The company specializes in food allergies and Graves’ disease, a condition that causes an overactive thyroid.

Deal: Priced at $18, the high end of its $16-18 range, upsized from 14.7 million to 17.64 million shares, ultimately raising about $318M.

Performance: +67% on Day 1.

Why it matters: The largest deal of a quiet month. Demonstrates investors are still willing to pay a premium for biotech companies despite the wider IPO index falling.

Watch out for: Lockup. This is when investors can begin selling their shares (180 days after the prospectus date, around April 2027) and its early results from human testing of its drugs, which will show if they are safe and working.

Bambusa Therapeutics (BBTX) · Listed on Nasdaq on 10/30

What they do: Developing antibody drugs, which are proteins that help the body fight disease for immunology and inflammatory diseases. The first candidate is aimed toward immune-related conditions.

Deal: Midpoint of its $15-17 range at $16, upsized from 9.38 million to 10.55 million shares, ultimately raising about $169M.

Performance: -6.3% on Day 1, closing at $14.99.

Why it matters: Despite up-sizing and mid-range pricing, Bambusa traded below its offer price.

Watch out for: How does the stock behave when it gets analyst coverage? Typically the banks involved in the deal (BofA, Evercore ISI, UBS and Cantor) will research the stock about 25 days after the IPO. This would put the first reports around 11/23 or 11/24. The initial ratings and price targets can be an early indication of how Wall Street views the stock.

Aspen Neuroscience (ASPE) · Listed on Nasdaq on 10/30

What they do: Developing a one-time cell therapy for Parkinson’s, that turns a patient’s own skin cells into new brain cells.

Deal: Low end of its $15-17 range at $15, sold 9.38 million shares as originally planned, raising about $140.6M.

Performance: -0.7% on Day 1, closing at $14.89.

Why it matters: The stock was priced at the low end of its range and still held near its offer price.

Watch out for: How does the stock move when it gets analyst coverage? Typically around 25 days after the IPO (around 11/23 or 11/24), the banks involved in the deal (Leerink, Piper Sandler, Stifel) will start to cover the stock. Aspen will be a good example to look at as it was at the low end of its range and finished day one near its $15 offer price.

Things to Watch Out For

Anthropic IPO: Anthropic submitted a public S-1 (the document a company must submit to register for an IPO). This was on 10/26; however, it has not yet established a price range or started trading. Anthropic is expected to have a mega IPO, and this, along with the upcoming midterm elections, complicates the IPO timeline into year-end. A deal this size could lift AI chip and cloud stocks, but also pull money from other sectors and crowd the IPO calendar.

Early post-listing performance: Both Aspen Neuroscience and Bambusa listed on 10/30. Their first few weeks of trading will offer an early, if limited, read on how late-month biotech IPOs hold up.

Lockup dates: Often, investors will not be able to sell their shares until an allotted time has passed. Usually, this is around 180 days after listing. Once that passes, there can be a flood of new sellers. Check the prospectus for exact dates.

Rates and AI sentiment: Both have affected IPO demand this fall. Any changes could quickly affect the calendar.

Startup Watch

Founders Spotlight

This edition highlights Step Ahead AI, a sports intelligence platform that uses artificial intelligence to turn basketball game film into actionable insight. Starting with high school programs across Ohio, Step Ahead AI gives teams at every level access to the kind of analysis once reserved for professional staffs.

What Step Ahead AI Does

Breaking down film, whether scouting an opponent or reviewing your own team, can take coaches hours of manual work each week. Most high school, club, and youth programs don’t have the staff or budget for dedicated analysts, so valuable information sits unused on game film. Step Ahead AI changes that. Coaches and players upload game film, and the platform’s AI analyzes it to produce detailed scouting reports on opponents along with specific improvement suggestions for players and teams in a fraction of the time it would take a coach.

“The average amount of time it takes the average athlete or coach to watch film or assess could be cut from hours into minutes,” said Nayak.

Coaches walk into games with a clearer plan, and players get feedback they can act on in practice.

Company Background

Founder Naveen Nayak began building Step Ahead AI in his home state of Washington nearly a year ago. Since then, the team has built a minimum viable product (MVP) and piloted it with more than 50 coaches and athletes across the country.

The company is now pursuing funding through student startup competitions at Ohio State’s Keenan Center, including the BOSS (Best of Student Startups) competition, the President’s Buckeye Accelerator, and the BuildX Accelerator. With a working product in coaches’ hands, Step Ahead AI is focused on growing within Ohio high school basketball.

Team

The team consists of three Ohio State students: Naveen Nayak, Suvir Reddy, and the Oval’s own, Charlie Slate.

Naveen is a freshman from Wenatchee, Washington, currently in the exploratory program. He is an intern for Coachable Plays, and involved with the Supply Chain Scholars program and the Entrepreneurship Living and Learning Community. Naveen is the Chief Executive Officer of the company.

Suvir is a freshman from Lewis Center, Ohio, majoring in Finance, with a minor in computer science engineering. He is a part of the Buckeye Capital Investors, the Buckeye Undergraduate Consulting Club, and Buckeye Fintech. Suvir is in charge of the product development and engineering.

Charlie Slate is a sophomore from Boston, Massachusetts, who is currently pursuing a major in Marketing and a minor in Business Analytics. He is involved on campus with the Alpha Kappa Psi business fraternity, the Sigma Eta Pi entrepreneurship fraternity, as well as being the VP of Marketing for the Oval Financial Forum. He drives the growth of the product as the Chief Marketing Officer.

Next Steps

In the next few months, Step Ahead has two big goals for itself: refining the product and growing the user base.

The team is iterating constantly based on conversations with coaches and players, so every update makes the platform more useful on game day and in practice.

Step Ahead AI aims to become the go-to scouting and player development tool for high school basketball, starting in Ohio. To do so, Step Ahead is showcasing demos at events like the Builders Showcase, using direct outreach to coaches, and offering free pilots for coaches and players alike.

OSU Student Impact

On October 29, Ohio State hosted the official launch event for BuildX, an initiative backed with a $1 million philanthropic fund, designed to help students interested in entrepreneurship move through the stages of the startup process. The event took place in Pomerene Hall, where 7,000 square feet of space has been set aside for BuildX. When asked about the newly created space, Associate Vice President for Student Innovation and Entrepreneurship, Shereen Agrawal noted that “this is a dedicated space for our student builders to be able to work, learn and connect with each other.” The broader entrepreneurial initiative has a goal of launching 100 investable student startups annually by 2030. Additionally, if selected to be a part of the program, teams may receive up to $15,000 in funding along with individualized coaching and access to a vast network of investors.

The event also highlighted the integration of the BuildX curriculum into Ohio State’s classes. Beginning in Spring 2027, students will have access to two three-credit-hour BuildX startup labs (BUSADM 4510.02, BUSADM 4510.03) along with the current Software Startup course (CSE 5251) that is already in place. These courses will give students the ability to apply concepts learned in the classroom to the business venture they are pursuing. This hands-on approach will be especially valuable to students interested in the intersection of business, technology, and engineering, allowing them to gain practical experience before entering the workforce.

In past years, one of the biggest obstacles many student entrepreneurs faced was the financial complications that came with developing a startup company from the ground up. BuildX directly addresses this barrier, allowing students to turn their ideas into a functioning business without having to worry about the financials associated with it. Additionally, direct access to experienced founders and investors can help students avoid common mistakes during the design process. Even for students who don’t necessarily envision themselves creating their own company, BuildX offers valuable opportunities for professional development. Building a startup often requires many of the skills and qualities companies look for in prospective employees, including financial planning, problem-solving and adaptability. Through BuildX, students will also gain experience in pitching ideas, evaluating business opportunities, and navigating decision making with scarce resources. These are all qualities that are highly valued in fields like consulting, high finance, venture capital and other business-related careers.

Ultimately, BuildX represents Ohio State’s continued commitment to supporting its students’ entrepreneurial ambitions. Alongside the new Keenan Center for Entrepreneurship on High Street, the initiative adds to a growing network of resources and opportunities available across campus. Rather than solely preparing students for traditional 9-to-5 careers after graduation, the university is equipping them with the tools to become the visionaries behind their own careers. Now, with the resources, funding, and connections that BuildX provides, students may no longer have to wait until graduation to turn their dreams into reality.
